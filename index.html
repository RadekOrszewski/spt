Dependency-aware sprint forecast

Feed it your throughput history and a backlog. It runs a Monte Carlo simulation over your dependency-discovery rate and delay length, then shows where each item is likely to land across the next few sprints — and how much capacity you should be holding back for dependencies nobody has found yet.

Recognised column headers: key, summary, points (or story points / estimate), depends on (or blocked by / dependency), labels, state
How labels are read

If a labels column is present, any label containing dep or blocked marks the item as having a known dependency. A label containing ready or resolved marks it as already satisfied. An explicit state column (none / waiting / ready) always wins.

3Forecast

Recalculates on every change
no known dependency known dependency, waiting known dependency, ready predecessor not scheduled earlier P85 capacity mark on the meter

The three cells under each item are its cumulative probability of being finished by the end of sprint 1, 2 and 3. A column's header confidence is the share of simulation runs in which everything planned up to and including that sprint had actually finished by the end of it. It is always far lower than any single item's number — that gap is the reason sprint commitments miss, and it is the figure worth quoting to a stakeholder.

4Method

Each run samples a sprint capacity from your throughput history (bootstrap, with replacement) rather than using an average, so a bad sprint stays possible. Items are pulled in backlog order until the sampled capacity runs out.

As each item is pulled, the model rolls for a dependency. An item marked waiting is delayed unless its predecessor finished in an earlier sprint. An item with no known dependency is delayed with probability equal to your late-discovery rate. Delay length is drawn from an exponential distribution with your measured mean, so most hits are short and a few are long — which is how dependency delays actually behave. A delayed item is pushed out by ceil(delay ÷ sprint length) sprints and frees its capacity for whatever is next in the backlog.

The reserve shown at the foot of each column is the analytical expectation — unlabelled points × discovery rate × (avg delay ÷ sprint length) — and is the amount of capacity you should leave uncommitted. It is not a buffer anyone owns; filling it with nice-to-have work makes the forecast meaningless.
