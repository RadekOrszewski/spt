Flow forecasting

Cross-team dependency forecast

Feed it what your team actually finished each sprint and a backlog labelled with the teams you depend on. It simulates the next few sprints against your measured discovery rate and each team's 85th-percentile response time, then shows where the work is likely to land — and how much capacity to hold back for the dependencies nobody has spotted yet.

The 85th percentile is the number you commit against: 85% of requests to that team come back within it. The simulation draws each delay from a distribution fitted to it, so most waits are shorter and a few are much longer. A team whose P85 exceeds your sprint length cannot be absorbed inside a sprint — those items are flagged on the board.

How teams and labels are read

The team column takes a name — Security, UX, anything. Names that are new get added to the team table with a placeholder P85 you can correct. A labels column containing dep or blocked marks the item as waiting; one containing ready or resolved marks the dependency as already satisfied. An explicit state column wins. An item marked as waiting with no named team is parked against Unassigned.

The three cells under each item are its cumulative probability of being finished by the end of sprints 1, 2 and 3. A column's header confidence is the share of simulation runs in which everything planned up to and including that sprint had actually finished by the end of it. It is always far lower than any single item's number — that gap is the reason sprint commitments miss, and it is the figure worth quoting to a stakeholder.

5What to start, what to raise

Capacity here is the level you hit at your confidence target, and each wait is that team's response time at the same level — at 85% it is exactly the P85 you entered. Raise-by dates are working days back from the sprint the item is scheduled into. This view is arithmetic, not simulation: it tells you what has to be true, while the board above tells you how likely it is.

7Method

Each run walks forward sprint by sprint. Every simulated sprint replays one real sprint drawn at random from your delivery history: its point total and its item count are both spent as the team works down the backlog in priority order. Sampling whole sprints rather than an average keeps the covariance between size and count intact — a sprint of six twos and a sprint of two eights are different shapes of capacity, and both stay possible.

The first time an item is picked up, the model rolls its dependency. An item labelled against a team waits on that team. An unlabelled item turns out to have a dependency with probability equal to your discovery rate, and the team it lands on is drawn in proportion to how often each team already appears in the labelled backlog. The wait itself is drawn from an exponential distribution fitted so its 85th percentile matches the figure you entered — mean = P85 ÷ 1.897 — which puts most waits well under the P85 and a long tail above it. The wait is assumed to start at a random point in the sprint, so a wait shorter than a sprint sometimes still lands inside it and sometimes tips over the edge — a team with a P85 of six days costs you far less than one with eighteen. Anything that tips over frees its capacity for whatever is next in the backlog.

The figure at the foot of each column is unlabelled points × discovery rate × (expected wait ÷ sprint length), where the expected wait is the weighted mean across the teams. It is a diagnostic showing how much of that column rests on dependencies nobody has spotted — the simulation has already accounted for it, so do not subtract it from capacity a second time.
